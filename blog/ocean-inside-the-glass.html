The Ocean Inside the Glass

For years, I lived with asthma. There were long stretches when every breath took effort. Breathing was not something that happened on its own. It was work, attention, and the small, continuous act that kept me here.

So I learned to sit with my breath—not as a spiritual practice, but as survival. I listened to it because I had to. I noticed every change, every tightness, and every moment when air became difficult to reach.

During one severe attack, something happened that I still remember with my whole body. My consciousness left it. There is a medical language for what happened: clinical death. For a moment, I was aware, and I was outside of myself. I could no longer experience reality in the ordinary way—as a body in a room, struggling for air and trying to remain present.

After that, my sense of reality was never quite the same. Not because I had read an idea somewhere, and not because someone had given me a spiritual explanation, but because I had touched something with my own being.

I want to be careful here. I am not offering my experience as proof of a theory, and I am not asking anyone to believe what I believe. I am only describing what happened to me, and the image that eventually helped me understand it.

Imagine a small amount of water inside a glass.

Inside the glass, the water knows the shape that holds it. It experiences itself through the limits of the container. The glass gives it form, edges, and a nameable boundary.

Now pour that same water into the ocean. Not one molecule changes. Nothing is added, and nothing is lost. Yet the scale of what it belongs to becomes impossible to measure.

The water is still the same water, but it is no longer defined by the smallness of the glass. That is the closest image I have for what happened to my sense of who I am: the same me, but no longer only the shape that had been holding me. An ocean instead of a glass.

What the word Starseed means to me

For me, this is what the word Starseed points toward—not the idea that we are better than other people, or that we should escape ordinary life. It is the quiet suspicion that consciousness may be larger than the form through which we currently experience it.

The body is real. The illness is real. The fear is real. The room, the breath, the limits, and the vulnerability are all real. But perhaps they are not the whole measure of what we are.

Perhaps the glass is not a prison. Perhaps it is simply the form we are learning through. And perhaps being a Starseed begins there—not with certainty, but with the feeling that something in us is wider than the life we have been taught to name.

The water does not need to hate the glass in order to remember the ocean.

It only needs to sense that the glass was never the whole story.
